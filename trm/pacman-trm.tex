\section{Overview}

The Pixel Array Control Monitor and Network (PACMAN) card is the warm electronics (located outside
of the cryostat) for the pixellated liquid argon (LAr) time projection chamber (TPC) based on the
LArPix ASIC.  The PACMAN card provides power and digital I/O to arrays of LArPix ASICs hosted on
pixel tile cards located within the cryostat.  In the production system, each PACMAN card handles up
to ten pixel tile cards.  The PACMAN card provides analog test and monitor signals as well as
digital control signals for clock, external trigger, reset, and sync.  The PACMAN card serves as a
timing system endpoint, hosts a Linux CPU, and is connected to the data acquisition system via
Ethernet.

The PACMAN card plays a supporting role in the LArPix system.  It does not face technical challenges
as severe as the cold electronics of the LArPix system and therefore a fundamental design constraint
is that it in no way limits the performance of the supported system.

\noindent
The PACMAN card (hardware version 1v5 described here) hosts a Trenz TE0720 module with a Xilinx
Zynq-7020 SoC (XC7Z020), which provides the processor (PS), the programmable logic (PL), the
Ethernet PHY, and memory.  The FPGA package pins of the PL signals are listed in
Appendix~\ref{sec:pins}.  The card features:
\begin{itemize}
\item Power input (J9):  48\,VDC, 2\,A, with a 2.5\,A fuse, reverse-polarity diode and a
  60\,V TVS.

\item Tile connector (J7):  a Samtec 400-position high-density connector (SEAFP) carrying power, LVDS I/O, and differential monitoring channels for the ten pixel tiles.


\item Ethernet (RJ45, from the PHY on the Trenz module), microSD card, and USB JTAG (TE0790 header).

\item Front panel:  two LEMO inputs (TRIGGER and PPS SYNC) and four U.FL jacks (TEST1/2 outputs,
  ISO-SIG0/1 inputs), all through digital isolators, and two SMA analog I/O jacks connected to the analog
  monitor multiplexers.

\item SFP cage with a clock and data recovery chip (ADN2814) for a timing system endpoint.

\item A 12-bit, 105\,Msps ADC (LTC2252) read by the PL (the ADC unit).

\item Monitoring and control over I2C from the PS:  two 16-channel DACs (AD5677R) setting VDDA and VDDD of
  each tile, six 4-channel power monitors (PAC1944), and two analog multiplexers (MAX14661).

\end{itemize}

\noindent
The PACMAN is powered from a single 48\,V input VIN (J9).  The rails derived from it are listed in
Table~\ref{tbl:hw-power} with their ratings and limits (schematic and datasheet values), and their
dependencies are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hw-rails}.  The two primary DC-DC converters, D3V6 and T3V0, are
powered directly from VIN.  The Trenz module is powered directly by D3V6.  The 3.3\,V I/O banks of the
Trenz, the LVDS drivers and receivers, the DACs, the power monitors and MUXes are all powered by
D3V3.  Each tile has its own T2V7 LDO, powering its own VDDA and VDDD LDOs with their output
voltages set by DAC channels at their NR/SS pins.

D3V6 is on when VIN is on, unless switched off by its over-temperature shutoff.  T3V0 is turned on
by ANALOG\_PWR\_EN, better called TILE\_POWER\_ENABLE, or by jumper J1.  Each tile's T2V7 is turned on
by its TILE\_ENABLE.  So a tile's power (VDDD and VDDA) requires both TILE\_POWER\_ENABLE and its own
TILE\_ENABLE.  Each VDDA and VDDD rail passes a 20\,m$\Omega$ shunt read by a power monitor, then a
common-mode choke, in series to the tile connector.

A single tile can draw up to 1\,A on VDDA and 1\,A on VDDD, and 2\,A in total, the rating of T2V7.
With all ten tiles powered from the 48\,V input, T3V0 allows 16.6\,A, an average of 1.66\,A per tile
for VDDA and VDDD together.  The highest VDDA or VDDD setting is limited by the dropout of its LDO
below T2V7: about 2.55\,V typical (2.46\,V worst case) at 1\,A.

The VDDA and VDDD outputs are set by the DACs (AD5677R, internal 2.5\,V reference, gain 1).  Each TPS7A94
sources a precise current, $I_{\rm NR/SS} = 150\,\mu$A, out of its NR/SS pin, through 1\,k$\Omega$ into the
DAC output, and regulates its output to the NR/SS voltage.  For a DAC code $D$ (16 bits):
$$ V_{\rm DD} = 2.5\,{\rm V} \times \frac{D}{65536} + 150\,\mu{\rm A} \times 1\,{\rm k}\Omega
             = 2.5\,{\rm V} \times \frac{D}{65536} + 0.150\,{\rm V}, $$
             that is, 156\,mV per \hex{1000}.
The TPS7A94 output is reliable above 0.4\,V (codes from about \hex{1A00}).  The highest useful setting is set by the dropout, as above.

\subsection{LVDS Signals}

Each tile has two LVDS quad drivers (SN65LVDS047, 3.3\,V) and one quad comparator (MAX964) as
receiver, all enabled by the tile's TILE\_ENABLE signal from the PL.  One LVDS driver sends the four
POSI lines, and the other LVDS driver sends the CLK and two additional ASIC control signals, which
we agnostically call G and H.  In the PACMAN schematics, G is called RST\_SYNC and H is called
TRIG, but when routed to a v3 ASIC G controls RESET and H muxes SYNC and TRIG.

The SN65LVDS047 is a current-mode driver: 2.5--4.5\,mA (3.1 typical), which gives its specified
swing of 250--450\,mV (310 typical) into 100\,$\Omega$, at a common mode of 1.17\,V.  On the board,
each driver output pair is loaded by a network of 36\,$\Omega$ from each line to a common node
(72\,$\Omega$ differential), and each line then passes an EMI filter (EMI7208, 100\,$\Omega$ series
with 8.5\,pF on each side) to the connector.  Each received pair passes the same EMI filter and is
terminated by 120\,$\Omega$ at the comparator.

In a physical loopback (POSI connected to PISO at the tile connector), the driver current divides between
the local 72\,$\Omega$ and the far path, $2 \times 100\,\Omega$ of filters and the 120\,$\Omega$ termination
(320\,$\Omega$):
\begin{align*}
  V_{\rm driver} &= I \times (72\,\Omega \parallel 320\,\Omega) = 3.1\,{\rm mA} \times 58.8\,\Omega \approx 180\,{\rm mV}, \\
  V_{\rm receiver} &= V_{\rm driver} \times \frac{120\,\Omega}{320\,\Omega} \approx 70\,{\rm mV}
\end{align*}
differential at the comparator (55--100\,mV over the driver's 2.5--4.5\,mA).

In normal operation the far end is the tile.  For reference, on the single-ASIC test card, each pair
into the ASIC (the four POSI, CLK, RESET and TRIG) is terminated by 100\,$\Omega$, and the POSI
pairs also see the ASIC's internal termination (1.25\,k$\Omega$ by default), about 93\,$\Omega$ in
all.  The pairs out of the ASIC (PISO) are not terminated on the card: their only termination is the
120\,$\Omega$ at the PACMAN comparator.  Table~\ref{tbl:hw-lvds} lists the resulting swings.

The LArPix-v3 PISO driver is also current-mode.  Its loop current is set by the ASIC configuration (the
number of transmit slices, tx\_slices, and the current per slice, i\_tx\_diff):  72\,$\mu$A with the default
settings, and at most 675\,$\mu$A.  Since the source is a current, the series filters do not reduce the
swing, which is the loop current times 120\,$\Omega$.  The MAX964 switches at input-referred trip points of
$\pm$2.0\,mV typical and so the margin of the PISO link is set by the ASIC's loop current
(Table~\ref{tbl:hw-lvds}).

\noindent
The PACMAN Zipline programmable logic (PL) firmware is
implemented in a hierarchical fashion, with six unit-level modules
that divide the PACMAN features as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item {\bf Global}: simple board-wide tasks. Sets the
  power and tile enables, configures the LEDs, reports the
  top-level status and firmware version.
\item {\bf TX}: configures, operates, and monitors the 40 UART transmitters.
\item {\bf RX}: configures, operates, and monitors the 40 UART receivers.
\item {\bf ATC}: generation of ASIC timing and control signals
  (clock, reset, sync, and trigger).  It produces a timestamp which the RX unit uses to record the arrival time of data.
\item {\bf ADC}: a one-differential-channel digital oscilloscope using the onboard high-performance ADC available starting with PACMAN 1v5.
\item {\bf LA}: a multi-channel logic analyzer for PISO/POSI/ATC and other signals of interest.
\item {\bf (CDR)}: clock and data recovery.  Reconstructs
  the $62.5~\rm MHz$ {\tt cdr\_clk} and timestamp.  This unit is planned for 3.7 but is not included in the 3.6 firmware.
\end{itemize}
These unit-level modules may appear to leave out some essential PACMAN
features, notably control of voltage levels, power monitoring, and
controlling the onboard multiplexer. These features are controlled
over I2C by the processor (PS), and are therefore not part of the PL
design. The top-level design of the PL firmware is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:pl-top}.  The corresponding Vivado block design (at
interface level) as presented by the Vivado GUI, is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:vivado-top}.  This version of the documentation is
synced with firmware version 3.6.\footnote{Generation of this
documentation inspired a few purely cosmetic changes (e.g. FIRST state
was renamed to START) to the firmware to reduce confusion.  So
technically, this documentation describes the not yet released 3.6.1
firmware.}  The latest release of 3.X firmware is available on GitHub
at
\begin{center} \url{https://github.com/mulhearn/pacman-firmware/tree/zipline-2023.2} \end{center}

The modules are implemented as custom RTL modules written in VHDL.
They contain submodules which are also written in VHDL.  Every module
adheres to project design rules, most importantly:
\begin{itemize}
 \item Custom RTL modules do not use any vendor IP.
 \item Each module can be compiled and simulated using the lightweight open-source ghdl tool, independently of any vendor tools.
 \item Each module {\tt xxx.vhd} has a testbench module {\tt tb/xxx\_tb.vhd} which demonstrates functionality to at least the level of initial hardware tests.
 \item Use a finite state machine (FSM) when it reduces complexity.
\end{itemize}


\noindent
Vendor IP, standard interfaces, and Block RAM are featured prominently in the design:
\begin{itemize}
 \item AXI-Lite (relatively slow 32-bit transfers) is used for custom registers.
 \item DMA (high performance direct memory access) is used for high-bandwidth TX and RX transfers.
 \item An AXI Stream FIFO is used to buffer the TX data between the DMA engine and the TX unit.
 \item An AXI Stream FIFO is used to buffer the RX data between the RX unit and the DMA engine.
 \item BRAM (Block RAM) is used by the ADC and LA units to store the time series of conversion data.
\end{itemize}
The AXI interface is widely used throughout the design, and is well
documented elsewhere.  A critical point for understanding the AXI
protocol is that data is transferred from a data producer to a data
consumer when and only when a beat occurs.  {\bf A beat is defined as
  a clock cycle during which the data producer asserts its valid
  signal and the data consumer asserts a ready signal.}
There are further requirements, but this is sufficient to understand
basic operation.  For conceptual economy, we use the same valid and
ready handshake elsewhere in the design, even when we do not implement
an AXI interface.  The design uses one custom interface: the register
bus (REGBUS).  Each unit-level module is a secondary on the REGBUS,
and responds on the clock cycle immediately following a read or write
request from the REGBUS primary, except that the RX and TX units add a
register stage for timing and acknowledge reads one cycle later.  The PS drivers
access the REGBUS interface via AXI-Lite.

\subsection{Clocks}

There are several logical clocks in the PACMAN firmware, which we define as:
\begin{itemize}
\item {\tt axi\_clk}: (100 MHz) AXI system clock, generated by the PS,
  always available.
\item {\tt cdr\_clk}: (62.5 MHz) generated by the Clock and Data Recovery
  (CDR) subsystem from external fiber-optic input, which might not be
  present. (Not implemented in 3.6)
\item {\tt sys\_clk}: (62.5--100 MHz) System clock, either from {\tt
  cdr\_clk} or generated by the PS, always available. (100 MHz in 3.6)
\end{itemize}

The 3.6 firmware does not include the CDR unit, and so the {\tt
  cdr\_clk} is not present, and the {\tt sys\_clk} is generated from
the PS, where it has been configured to 100 MHz.  After the release of
3.6, a consensus was finally reached that {\tt sys\_clk} would be set
to operate at 62.5 MHz (direct from {\tt cdr\_clk} or generated by the
PS), much to the delight of the system designers.  As detailed in the
ATC unit, the PACMAN firmware treats the ASIC clock (UCLK) as a signal in the
{\tt sys\_clk} domain.

As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:domains}, firmware components run in one of
two clock domains: {\tt axi\_clk} and {\tt sys\_clk}. The firmware
handles clock domain crossing for signals that pass between these two
domains.  A register read or write is initiated on the PS in the {\tt
  axi\_clk} domain.  The register bus includes a CDC stage to reach
all modules in the {\tt sys\_clk} domain.  The TX and RX units are in
the {\tt sys\_clk} domain, and reach the DMA engine, in the {\tt
  axi\_clk} domain, via FIFOs.  Lastly, the global unit is in the {\tt
  axi\_clk} domain.

\subsection{Registers}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.68\paperwidth, keepaspectratio]{timing_diagrams/single_write.pdf}
\caption{Single REGBUS write request (output of ghdl testbench)}
\label{fig:single-write}
\end{figure}

\noindent
Access to registers in the PL firmware is done via a simple custom
register bus (REGBUS).  A REGBUS write request is made by the single
primary setting the 16-bit write address to that of the register of
interest, the 32-bits of write data to the desired value, and the
write update bit high.  The secondary on the bus responsible for that
register responds by setting the write acknowledge bit high and
performing any other desired effect, such as setting a register to the
value of the write data.  A single write request is illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:single-write}.  Command registers are implemented by
setting a command signal high for one clock cycle after a write to the
command register address, sometimes ignoring the write data entirely.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.68\paperwidth, keepaspectratio]{timing_diagrams/single_read.pdf}
\caption{Single REGBUS read request (output of ghdl testbench)}
\label{fig:single-read}
\end{figure}

A read request on the bus is made by the single primary setting the
16-bit read address to that of the register of interest and setting
the read update bit high.  The secondary on the bus responsible for
that register responds by setting the 32-bit read data to the value
associated with the register of interest, and setting the read
acknowledge bit high.  A single read request is illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:single-read}.

The register bus is implemented by the module {\tt axil\_to\_regbus.vhd}
which translates AXI-Lite requests from the PS to read and write
requests on the REGBUS.  Because FPGAs do not have internal tristate
buffers, the bus implementation also requires a multiplexer {\tt
  regbus\_hub.vhd} to connect the REGBUS primary to multiple
secondaries.

Within the same clock domain, REGBUS requests
are acknowledged on the next clock cycle, as shown in
Figs.~\ref{fig:single-write} and \ref{fig:single-read}, except reads
of the RX and TX units, which add a register stage for timing and
acknowledge one cycle later.  In addition, a
CDC stage in the register bus {\tt regbus\_cdc} introduces
synchronization delays, and so the acknowledgement from units in the
{\tt sys\_clk} domain is not seen in the {\tt axi\_clk} domain until
several clock cycles later.  For this reason, the read address, write
address, and write data are held fixed until the acknowledgement is
received, or a timeout occurs.  The timeout ensures that writing an
invalid address does not hang the AXI-Lite bus.  Read responses to an
invalid address return \hex{EEEEEEEE}.  (Additional error flags for
invalid addresses are available, but not fully implemented in version
3.6.)

The 16-bit address space for PACMAN registers is allocated in a
hierarchical fashion, represented as
$$0{\rm x}SRRR$$
with the most significant four bits $0{\rm x}S$ defining the scope, and the
remaining 12 bits $0{\rm x}RRR$ defining the specific register within the scope.
For example, the scratch A register (0x020), of the global unit (0xF)
would be found at the 16-bit address:
$$0{\rm xF}020.$$
The scopes are allocated as shown in Table~\ref{tbl:scope}.

\noindent
The global unit handles simple board-wide tasks, and manages the registers listed in
Table~\ref{tbl:global-reg}.  The bit field interpretations for some registers are shown in
Table~\ref{tbl:global-bit-fields}.

The read-only registers can be used to determine the firmware version installed, the hardware
version the firmware was configured for, the date and time, git hash, and Vivado version used at
firmware synthesis.  The read-write scratch registers SCRATCH\_A and SCRATCH\_B have no impact
beyond storing and retrieving the register value, and are useful for testing the operation of the
AXI-Lite and REGBUS interfaces.

Providing power and data I/O to the tiles requires enabling power to the tile power subsystem
(called ANALOG\_PWR\_EN in hardware schematics) as well as enabling the individual tile.  These
settings are available in the ENABLES register, using the bit fields TILE\_POWER\_ENABLE and
TILE\_ENABLES.  While a tile is disabled, even with tile power enabled, the LVDS transmitters and
receivers are off, and both VDDD and VDDA are at zero. Setting the values of VDDD and VDDA, and
monitoring currents and voltages on the board, is done over I2C, by the PS, and so is not part of
the PL firmware design.

The PACMAN has two LEDs controlled by the PL.  Their behavior is controlled by the LEDS register.
In the current firmware, each LED is simply turned on or off directly, by the LED-2 and LED-3 bits,
but in the future, they could be driven by specific conditions as well.  A timing diagram
demonstrating control of the LED and enable outputs over the register bus is shown in
Fig.~\ref{fig:global}.  Two additional LEDs are driven from the PS, and are not part of the PL
firmware design.  Two LEDs on the Trenz module are also controllable from the PS.  The global unit
also provides a global status register (STATUS).  In version 3.6, the status register is implemented
as a fixed pattern.

The ATC unit is highly configurable and agnostic about the role of signals it produces.  Inputs and
outputs are given generic names instead of functional names, which reinforces the agnostic design
and allows different generations of PACMAN hardware to use the same firmware.  The heart of the
module is the ATC\_MUX, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:atc-mux}, which generates outputs based on its
configuration and input stimuli.  The available input stimuli are:
\begin{itemize}
 \item LEMO A--B are the two LEMO inputs (for example a PPS or external trigger).  They are
  double-flop synchronized with a configurable polarity.  Each rising (or falling if polarity is
  inverted) edge that holds for more than one clock cycle produces a single clock cycle input
  stimulus.
 \item POKE A--D are single clock cycle stimuli that fire upon a register write.  They also carry a
   tile mask, which can be used to specify the tiles that receive the signal per request.
 \item LOGIC A--B are logical inputs, not yet implemented.
\end{itemize}
The stimuli can be configured to generate signals at the available outputs:
\begin{itemize}
 \item G outputs (one per tile) with configurable duration, driving the ASIC RESET on the PACMAN 1v5 (schematic net SYNC/RST).
 \item H outputs (one per tile) with configurable duration, driving the ASIC SYNC and TRIG on the PACMAN 1v5 (schematic net TRIG).
 \item Marker 0--3: these single clock cycle pulses are sent to the RX unit, where they can cause
   markers to be added to the event record (such as recording an external trigger). Marker 0 is also connected to the timestamp reset.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
The TX unit is responsible for configuration, operation, and
monitoring of transmission to the tile cards via 40 UART channels.  A
block diagram of the TX unit is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tx-unit}.  The
data to be transmitted arrives as an AXI stream, filled by the PS
using DMA. A FIFO carries the stream from the {\tt axi\_clk} domain to
the {\tt sys\_clk} domain.  The TX buffer module receives the stream
data and holds it until it has been handed off to the TX channel
modules for transmission.  It has both an input buffer and an output
buffer, so that it can receive new stream data while the output is
being held constant.

from inactive UARTs are simply not used.

{\bf TX buffer:} As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tx-stream}, when the FIFO
is non-empty, the TX buffer receives stream data by asserting {\tt
  tready}, shifting each word into its 41-word buffer until the DMA
packet is complete, as indicated by {\tt tlast}.  The TX buffer
then deasserts {\tt tready}, halting the stream, until the new packet
has been moved to its output buffer, at which point it asserts {\tt
  tready} again.  Holding {\tt tready} low is how the TX unit adds
back pressure to DMA.  Fig.~\ref{fig:tx-backpressure} shows the TX
unit operating the UART transmission at full speed.\footnote{Even when
the output buffer is empty, as in Fig.~\ref{fig:tx-stream} and for
the first two packets of Fig.~\ref{fig:tx-backpressure}, there are
three clock cycles during which {\tt tready} is low.  This unneeded
latency is the result of a design that registers signals at the stream
interface as well as the state of the TX buffer state machine.  Since
the transmission rate is ultimately limited by the UART, this is an
intentional design choice that favors simplicity and robustness at no
cost to performance.}

The transfer of data between the TX buffer and each TX channel is
coordinated with {\tt uvalid} and {\tt uready} signals (one for each
UART channel).  As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tx-stream}, when a new
packet arrives at the TX buffer output, {\tt uvalid} is asserted for
each channel enabled in the UART mask from the first beat of the
packet.  Each TX channel has a 64-bit shift register for transmission,
and it asserts {\tt uready} for one clock cycle just as the start bit
begins and the new input has been loaded into its shift
register. The TX buffer deasserts {\tt uvalid} on the next clock
cycle, marking the data for that channel as consumed.  The TX buffer
holds its output buffer constant until every enabled UART has consumed
its data, and so every {\tt uvalid} is low.  At that point, it shifts
its next input buffer into the output buffer if one is available, and
the process repeats.  As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tx-backpressure},
after initial buffering, it is the withholding of {\tt uready} that
holds the output buffer in the TX buffer, keeping {\tt tready} deasserted,
and throttling the DMA engine to match the transmission rate.\\[4pt]

{\bf TX channel:}
The UART transmission is handled by 40 instances of the TX Channel
module, with one assigned to each UART channel.  The UART timing is
controlled by the configurable BAUD strobe of the ATC unit, one clock
cycle before the start of each bit period (every 10 clock cycles, or 10
Mbit/s, by default in 3.6).  Bit transitions occur PHASE + 1 clock
cycles after the start of each bit period (PHASE + 2 clock cycles
after the BAUD strobe).  While idle, the line is high.

At the first transition during which valid data is offered (uvalid),
the channel loads the word into its shift register, asserts uready for
one clock cycle, and sends the start bit (low).  It then sends the 64
data bits, least significant first, then the stop bit (high).  It then
waits DELAY further bit periods, with the line high, before it may
load the next word.  As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:tx-backpressure}, full
speed (back-to-back frames) transmission is reached for DELAY=0, for
as long as data is present.

At 10 MHz (100 ns), transmitting 64 bits, plus a
start and stop bit, requires 6600 ns, so that the maximum TX
throughput across 40 UART channels is:
$$R = \frac{40}{66 \cdot 100~{\rm ns}} = 6060~{\rm packets}/{\rm ms}$$
The DUNE detector clock is $62.5~\rm MHz$ (16 ns).  With a baud period
of 8 clock cycles, each bit takes $128~{\rm ns}$ and the maximum TX throughput is:
$$R = \frac{40}{66 \cdot 128~{\rm ns}} = 4735~{\rm packets}/{\rm ms}$$
The TX firmware has been benchmarked in the hardware (using both
bare-metal applications and Linux) to reach maximum speed.\\[4pt]

{\bf Registers:} The registers of the TX unit are assigned the range
of scopes (upper four bits of the 16-bit register address) from 0x0 to 0x3.  Each
16-bit address within the TX unit scope is interpreted as:
$${\rm 0b00}cc cccc rrrr rrrr$$ where $C=0bcc cccc$ is the 6-bit
channel index, and $R=0brrrr rrrr$ is the 8-bit register.  The 40 UART
channels are assigned $C=0$ to $C=39=0x27$.  In-common registers
(not channel-specific) are assigned to logical channel $C=0x3F$.  For
convenience, register writes to broadcast channel $C=0x3B$ update
every UART channel's register to the same value.  Broadcast reads are
not supported. See Table~\ref{tbl:tx-scope} for a summary.

The registers of the TX unit are listed in Tables~\ref{tbl:tx-reg}
and~\ref{tbl:tx-common}, and bit fields in Table~\ref{tbl:tx-bit-fields}.
The configuration of each UART (via
UART\_CONFIG) holds the PHASE and DELAY parameters.  The BAUD signal
is provided by the ATC unit and configured there.

The UART status register provides a snapshot of each channel for one
clock cycle.  Counters derived from the status are available per
channel: the number of start bits (UART\_STARTS) and the number of
uvalid and uready beats (UART\_BEATS).  The 16-bit counts saturate at
0xFFFF and are cleared at reset or by writing the zero counts
(ZERO\_CNTS) register.  A look register is provided to support reading
the TX buffer's output buffer:  the words of the packet being
transmitted, or of the last packet once transmission is complete.  A
packet still waiting in the input buffer is not shown, and each new
packet replaces the word of every UART, including those not enabled by
its mask.  To reduce FPGA resources, access is through a MUX that
selects one UART channel at a time.  The look registers (LOOK\_LOWER
and LOOK\_UPPER) report the 64-bit word (lower and upper bits) of the
UART channel selected via LOOK\_SELECT.  To be clear, LOOK\_SELECT can be used to
read (in series) every channel after a transmission has already
occurred (it need not be selected ahead of time).

\noindent
The RX unit receives UART packets from the tile cards and streams them
to the PS.  Each 64-bit UART packet is bundled within a 192-bit PACMAN
word, as defined in Table~\ref{tbl:pacman_word_fields}, consisting of
three 64-bit fragments: a header, a receipt timestamp, and the UART
packet.  The header contains the word type (data or config), the
PACMAN ID, and the UART channel.  Together with the chip ID contained
in the UART payload, the unique ASIC chip that originated the packet is
completely specified.

A block diagram of the RX unit is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:rx-unit}.
Each incoming 64-bit UART packet, enclosed within a start and stop bit
defining a 66-bit frame, is deserialized by one of 40 independently
operating RX channels, which registers the payload and its arrival
timestamp into its output buffer.  The RX buffer visits each channel
in turn, and, for each waiting payload, sends a 192-bit PACMAN word
(as three 64-bit fragments) to an AXI stream.  To reduce congestion,
the RX buffer selects the payload and timestamp via a registered MUX.
A FIFO crosses the stream into the {\tt axi\_clk} domain, and the AXI
DMA engine writes the packets to memory accessible from the PS.

{\bf RX channel:} The ASIC clock (UCLK) is driven by the PACMAN as a signal
in the {\tt sys\_clk} domain, with a fixed phase relative to the UART
and baud strobes.  The PISO signal therefore has a fixed phase with
respect to the baud strobe, but as it passes through an unspecified
cable length, that phase is unknown.  The RX channel is designed to
support both a traditional edge-detect UART mode and a fixed sample
phase mode.  The traditional UART mode treats the PISO as if it is
from a different clock domain.  The PISO signal is passed through a
double-flop synchronizer to reduce metastability risk, and the sample
phase is set at a fixed delay past the falling edge detected during
idle.  The fixed sample phase mode relies on knowledge of the location
of a good phase relative to the baud strobe for sampling far from any
edge.  This mode needs neither the CDC double-flop synchronizer nor
the edge detection (even though both are in place) and is therefore
the most deterministic once a correct setting is determined.  The two
modes differ only in how the sample flag (which specifies at which
point the PISO signal should be sampled per bit) is determined.

A timing diagram for an RX channel receiving the start of a new frame
is presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:rx-chan}.  The receiver runs faster than
the baud rate, as marked by the baud strobe ({\tt baud}), which
provides a consistent reference for each bit period in the {\tt
  sys\_clk} domain.  The RX channel has an input MUX that selects
between PISO (normal operation), the channel's own TX output (POSI
loopback), a test pattern, no input (the line held high), or (when
available) the output of an FPGA-emulated ASIC.  The MUX-selected
input (POSI in the timing diagrams) feeds a three stage tap ({\tt
  tap(0)} to {\tt tap(2)}).  The tap is used to determine a majority
vote ({\tt vote}) across three clock cycles, with non-unanimous votes
marked by the {\tt dissent} signal.  A falling edge signal is asserted
only for the clean falling edge pattern 001.

The RX channel is a finite state machine, with states:
\begin{itemize}
  \item {\bf IDLE:} Waiting on the start bit.
  \item {\bf START:} Start bit received, waiting on first payload bit.
  \item {\bf DATA:} First payload bit received, waiting on remaining payload bits.
  \item {\bf STOP:} Waiting on stop bit.
\end{itemize}
Each bit is sampled from the majority vote on the clock cycle that the
sample strobe ({\tt sample}) is asserted. After the bit or bits that
each state is waiting on have been sampled, the state machine advances
to the next state.  The frame ends when the stop bit is detected with
the expected value of 1.  The RX channel registers the 64-bit payload
at its output, along with its arrival timestamp (latched at the stop
bit), and asserts {\tt uvalid}, marking the payload as ready for
streaming.  Once the RX buffer takes the word into its holding
register, it asserts {\tt uready}, the RX channel deasserts {\tt
  uvalid}, and the output buffer is ready to receive the next packet.

The RX channel outputs a status register that includes {\tt uvalid}
and {\tt uready} (the logical AND of the two is called a beat), a start
flag that is asserted once per detected start bit, and an update flag
that is asserted once per detected stop bit.  During normal operation,
the number of starts, updates, and beats should all be equal.  The
status register also includes error flags that fire for one clock
cycle when an error condition is detected.  If {\tt uvalid} is high
and {\tt uready} is not being asserted on the cycle that a new packet
has been received, the new packet is discarded, and the {\tt lost}
flag is asserted for one clock cycle.  If the stop bit is invalid (a
zero instead of one), the new packet is discarded, and the {\tt frame
  error} flag is asserted for one clock cycle.  The dissent flag is
expected for two clock cycles during any bit transition, but the
sample cycle should be away from transitions when configured for
normal operation.  A noise flag is set when dissent occurs at a sample
point.  Under normal operation, lost packets, frame errors, and noisy
samples should not occur.  The RX unit diagnostic registers (described
below) include counters for starts, beats, updates, lost packets, frame
errors, and noisy samples.  A timing diagram showing the reception of four
back-to-back packets, including a noisy sample, a frame error, and a
lost packet is presented in Fig.~\ref{fig:rx-chan-frames}.

{\bf RX buffer:} The RX buffer streams UART data in round-robin
fashion, following a turn counter that cycles through each channel.
Each turn lasts for three clock cycles, so that three 64-bit fragments
(header, timestamp, and payload) can be streamed per turn, together forming one 192-bit
PACMAN word per payload received.  The RX buffer is an FSM with the
following states:
\begin{itemize}
  \item {\bf IDLE:}  waiting for arrival of valid data.\\
     $\Rightarrow$ On arrival of valid data, move to {\bf STREAM} state.

  \item {\bf WAIT\_STATE:} a complete turn sweep is done, wait for new data or timeout\\
     $\Rightarrow$ On arrival of valid data, move to {\bf STREAM} state\\
     $\Rightarrow$ If configurable timeout occurs, move to {\bf TRAILER} state

  \item {\bf STREAM:} turn-based round-robin streaming of data to FIFO. Each RX
     channel is assigned one turn which lasts long enough to stream
     three 64-bit fragments when valid data is available and the
     stream is not busy.\\
     $\Rightarrow$ If the number of words sent reaches a threshold, move to
     {\bf TRAILER} state.  The next packet resumes the sweep at the
     channel whose turn was cut short, so that busy low channels cannot
     starve the higher ones.\\
     $\Rightarrow$ After a complete turn sweep, move to {\bf WAIT\_STATE}

  \item {\bf TRAILER:} stream trailer word.
     At the end of the packet, send three 64-bit fragments to write a
     192-bit trailer reporting the number of 192-bit words streamed
     (not including the TRAILER), and set LAST bit on the final
     fragment of the trailer.\\
     $\Rightarrow$ Return to {\bf IDLE} state after TRAILER is streamed.
\end{itemize}
A timing diagram of the RX buffer in action is presented in
Fig.~\ref{fig:rx-stream}.  The arrival of valid data on UART channels
0, 1, and 3 moves the FSM from IDLE to STREAM state.  During {\tt turn}
0, the payload and timestamp for UART channel 0 is selected at the
MUX. During {\tt turn} 1, the payload and timestamp for UART channel 1 is
selected at the MUX, while the header, timestamp, and payload for UART channel 0
are streamed out, during {\tt fragment} 0,1 and 2.  Due to pipeline
delays, each fragment arrives in {\tt tdata} two cycles later.  This
process continues, skipping channel 2, which has no valid data, until
channel 3 is streamed.

For this simulation, the maximum number of words per packet is set to
three, so the FSM moves to TRAILER once the third word has been
streamed, and then streams the trailer word (an ASCII L character and
the number of words (3) not counting the trailer, plus padding).  The
last fragment of the trailer is marked with {\tt tlast}, marking the
end of the DMA packet, and the FSM returns to the IDLE state.  The
size of the DMA packet is configurable based on a maximum number of
words (three in the scenario above) and a timeout in units of 256
clock cycles, counted from the start of the packet.  In both cases, a
zero is no condition (no timeout / no maximum).  The timeout is not
checked while in stream state, so a
timeout of one (default setting) will send data from all channels with
valid data once valid data from at least one channel is available.

Besides the 40 UARTs, the RX buffer serves four additional channels
for heartbeats, rollover, and trigger (or other) markers from the ATC.

The registers of the RX unit are listed in Tables~\ref{tbl:rx-reg} and~\ref{tbl:rx-common}, and bit
fields in Tables~\ref{tbl:rx-bit-fields} and~\ref{tbl:rx-buffer-bit-fields}.  The configuration of each UART (via UART\_CONFIG) holds
the PHASE, MODE, and PERIOD parameters, the input select bits, and the ACCEPT\_DISSENT flag.  If no
input (or more than one input) is specified, the line is set high, disabling the UART.  The BAUD
signal is provided by the ATC unit and configured there.

The {\tt UART\_CONFIG} selects between the fixed-sample phase mode and the edge-detect mode.  In the
fixed-sample phase mode, the {\tt PHASE} field places the sample strobe PHASE + 1 clock cycles after
the start of the bit period (PHASE + 2 clock cycles after the baud strobe).  In the edge-detect
mode, the sample strobe occurs PHASE + 2 clock cycles after the falling edge of the start bit (at
the selected input) and once per baud period thereafter.  For normal operation, PERIOD should be set
to the baud period, and PHASE should be less than the baud period $-$ 2.  Four input select bits
choose the input (PISO, POSI loopback, test pattern, or emulator), one bit set at a time.  If no bit
is set, or more than one, the line is held high, which effectively disables the UART.  A permissive
flag (ACCEPT\_DISSENT) allows dissent to trigger a start bit.

The RX buffer configuration register (BUFFER\_CONFIG) includes the
TIMEOUT and MAX\_WORDS fields, which control the end of packet
determination as described above.

A look register is provided to support reading the latest data
received by each UART channel independently of DMA.  To reduce FPGA
usage, access is via a second output channel of the same MUX used by
the RX buffer.  The look registers (LOOK\_LOWER and LOOK\_UPPER)
report the most recent 64-bit payload (lower and upper bits) of the
UART channel selected via LOOK\_SELECT.

Several counts are maintained for each UART: the number of start bits
received (UART\_STARTS), beats observed (UART\_BEATS), and successful
receptions (UART\_UPDATES).  The number of lost packets (UART\_LOST),
as indicated by the lost bit being asserted, is likewise counted, as are
framing errors (UART\_FRAME\_ERR) and noisy samples (UART\_NOISE).
The 16-bit counts saturate at 0xFFFF and are cleared upon reset and by
writing the zero counts (ZERO\_CNTS) register.  The current number of
words (FIFO\_CNT) in the RX FIFO is available, as is the maximum number of words (FIFO\_MAX)
since the last reset or zero counts command.

The RX unit dynamically determines the packet type from bits 1--0 of
each ASIC payload (the first two bits received).  The behavior is specified by the
WORD\_TYPE\_LUT, which includes 4 bytes for the desired output
for 00, 01, 10, and 11 (bits 7--0 for 00, \ldots, bits 31--24 for 11).  The current default is 0x44444444 which sets all
ASIC packets to DATA type (0x44), but this feature can be used to sort
packets into CONFIG and DATA types.

The Zynq SoC hosts an ARM processor capable of running either Linux (including custom PACMAN
software) or bare-metal applications.  The software design is centered around Linux, but the project
fully supports bare-metal applications (avoiding Linux) as a workflow for when a tighter development
cycle is needed.

The PACMAN vertical integration stack is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pacman-stack}.  The hardware and
firmware were described in Sections~\ref{sec:hardware} and \ref{sec:firmware}.  This documentation
describes the PACMAN software, based on the 3.6.4 version (not yet released).  The latest PACMAN
software is available as a GitHub repository:
\begin{center} \url{https://github.com/mulhearn/pacman-software/tree/zipline-2023.2} \end{center}
Paths in this section are relative to \texttt{petalinux/pkg/pacman-apps/files/} unless they start
with \texttt{apps/} (the bare-metal applications at the top of the repository).  {\bf Unlike the
  hardware and firmware, which are mature and evolving more incrementally and predictably, the
  PACMAN software layer is still under rapid development, and is still missing some of the crucial
  features needed for a reliable system.}

The primary role of the PACMAN software is to stand up the interface to the DUNE Data Acquisition
(DAQ) system, as presented in Section~\ref{sec:daq-interface}.  The implementation of this
interface, through drivers and software applications running on the ARM processor, is described in
subsequent sections.  The {\tt pacman\_cmdserver} handles requests to read and write PACMAN
registers and to transmit packets to the ASICs, while the {\tt pacman\_dataserver} receives and
publishes packets from the ASICs.  The {\tt pacman\_menu} is an ASCII menu-driven interface for
expert monitoring, special configurations, and testing.

\noindent
The DAQ reaches the PACMAN over Ethernet, through three ZMQ sockets bound by the two servers
(Table~\ref{tbl:sockets}).  The REP socket serves one request at a time: a client sends a request
and waits for its reply before the next.  The PUB sockets are fire and forget.  Each ZMQ message
adheres to the PACMAN message format (version 1.1) consisting of a 24-byte header
(Table~\ref{tbl:pacman_header_fields} and Fig.~\ref{fig:pacman_header_layout}) followed by $N$ bytes
holding 24-byte PACMAN words or the text (string) of a high-level command or reply.  The PACMAN
message format is defined in \texttt{include/pacman\_message.hh} (C++, used by the servers and the
board tools) and \texttt{pylib/pacman\_message.py} (Python, used by \texttt{utils/pacman\_util.py}
and by clients).  Multi-byte fields are little-endian.

The sequence and session fields (bytes 16--23, new in version 1.1) are currently used by DATA
messages only and are zero otherwise.  The session is a random non-zero number drawn when
\texttt{pacman\_dataserver} starts, and the sequence counts the messages of that session from 0
(wrapping at $2^{32}$).  A client detects a lost message by a gap in the sequence, and a restart of
the data server by a new session.  The timestamp of a REPLY is copied from its REQUEST.  A REQUEST
holds up to 16000 words (\texttt{MAX\_WORDS}).  The command server checks that the message is at
least a header long, no larger than its buffer, and exactly as long as the header says.  A request
that fails a check is answered by a REPLY of one ERR word (error code \hex{EEEE}).  Otherwise the
reply has one word per request word, in the same order.

A primary role of the PACMAN card is delivering configuration packets to the ASICs and returning
their replies.  The DATA and CFG word types contain ASIC packets as their payloads, and they are
delivered and received by the PACMAN to and from the ASICs as is.  The PACMAN is largely agnostic
about the details.  The latest firmware does include a lookup table that allows the PACMAN to
discern whether a packet is a data or a configuration packet, so that its PACMAN message word type
can be set to DATA or CFG as appropriate.  As of 3.6.4, all DATA and CFG words are sent to the same
ZMQ PUB socket, but in the future, CFG words could be sent to a dedicated configuration-only stream.

The READ and WRITE word types refer to a virtual register space that provides both direct and
indirect access to the PACMAN firmware AXI-Lite register space and I2C peripherals, within the
regions defined in Table~\ref{tbl:regions}.  The decoding is handled in the software by
\texttt{pacman\_read} and \texttt{pacman\_write} (\path{src/drivers-linux/pacman_vspace.cc}, with regions defined in \path{include/addr_conf.hh}).  Channels and tiles in virtual register arguments are
zero-referenced (tile 1 is 0, UART 0 is 0).  The Hardware registers provide direct access to the
firmware registers of the AXI-Lite register bus interface at base address \hex{40000000}.  For
example, to read the firmware major version (FIRMWARE\_MAJOR, Table~\ref{tbl:global-reg}), one can
run, on the PACMAN:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
root@Trenz:~# utils/pacman_util.py --read 0xFF10
\end{lstlisting}
which is the same as reading the hardware register directly:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
root@Trenz:~# devmem 0x4000FF10
\end{lstlisting}
Virtual registers do not have an equivalent \texttt{devmem} command.  The PACMAN virtual registers are write only, and are used for configuration that is not strictly tied to the firmware registers, following Table~\ref{tbl:vregs}.  For example, to enable the main analog power and then all ten
tiles (the written value is not used):
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
root@Trenz:~# utils/pacman_util.py --write 0x00100114 0 --write 0x001002F4 0
\end{lstlisting}

The I2C virtual registers provide read and write access to I2C peripherals, following
Table~\ref{tbl:i2cregs}.  For example, to read the VDDA voltage (mV) and current (mA) of tile 1:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
root@Trenz:~# utils/pacman_util.py --read 0x00200030 --read 0x00200050
\end{lstlisting}

\noindent
The PACMAN applications rely on PACMAN-specific user-space drivers, which implement low-level PACMAN
features such as DMA and AXI-Lite register access.  Bare-metal and Linux applications run on
fundamentally different platforms. Bare-metal applications access hardware and memory directly at
their physical addresses, while Linux applications run in virtual memory, so the hardware registers
and DMA buffers must first be mapped into the application's address space (with \texttt{mmap} of
\texttt{/dev/mem}).  Linux hardware drivers (such as for I2C) are also different from the
Xilinx-provided hardware drivers for bare-metal applications.

The PACMAN driver code relies on a platform layer that defines the lowest level interface to the
hardware, with separate Linux and bare-metal implementations.  The platform layer
(\path{src/platform/hw_access.h}) declares all access to the hardware: AXI-Lite
registers, DMA registers and buffers, I2C, PS GPIO, a timer and menu input.  Under Linux it maps the
hardware from \texttt{/dev/mem} (root required) and uses the kernel's I2C and GPIO drivers.  Bare
metal uses the Xilinx standalone drivers.

The PACMAN-specific common drivers access the hardware only through this interface, so that the same
code can be shared between bare-metal and Linux platforms.  The Linux applications and their
(user-space) drivers are built atop these common drivers.  The hierarchical driver structure is
illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:drivers}.

\section{Developer Workflows}\label{sec:workflows}

This section describes common development workflows based on Vivado (and PetaLinux) version 2023.2,
running under Ubunutu Linux.  Additional documentation is available in each repository under
\texttt{docs/flow.txt}.  Typical setup of the development tools:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
source /tools/Xilinx/Vivado/2023.2/settings64.sh
source /tools/Xilinx/PetaLinux/2023.2/tool/settings.sh
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Building Firmware and Software from the Repositories}

Firmware, from \texttt{pacman-firmware/}.  The hardware version is chosen at project creation:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
cd vivado
vivado -mode batch -source tcl/create.tcl -tclargs hw1v5
vivado -mode batch -source tcl/runsib.tcl
cd ..
\end{lstlisting}
The hardware export file is \texttt{products/pacman.xsa}.  Software, from
\texttt{pacman-software/}:  the hardware export, then the first-stage boot loader, then the
PetaLinux image:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
cp <pacman-firmware>/products/pacman.xsa hardware/
xsct apps/pacman_fsbl/install.tcl
cd petalinux
./scripts/create_full.sh
cd full
../scripts/config.sh
../scripts/build.sh
../scripts/package.sh
\end{lstlisting}
\texttt{BOOT.BIN}, \texttt{image.ub} and \texttt{boot.scr} (in \texttt{images/linux}) go on the
microSD card's FAT partition, and \texttt{rootfs.tar.gz} is extracted (as root) to its Linux
partition.

\subsection{Firmware Development}

HDL is developed and validated in \texttt{ghdl/}, without vendor tools.  Every module has a
testbench, for example for the TX unit:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
cd ghdl/tx
make
make run-testbenches
cd ../..
\end{lstlisting}
Validated HDL is copied into the Vivado source tree, and the project is created again:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
./utils/sync_ghdl.sh
cd vivado
rm -fr pacman-fw
vivado -mode batch -source tcl/create.tcl -tclargs hw1v5
vivado -mode batch -source tcl/runsib.tcl
cd ..
\end{lstlisting}
The new \texttt{products/pacman.xsa} then goes through the software build above, from
\texttt{config.sh} on (the PetaLinux project need not be created again).

\subsection{Software Application Development}

For the PACMAN, PetaLinux cross-compiles the \texttt{pacman-apps} package alone, and packages it as a
tarball (from \texttt{petalinux/full}):
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
../scripts/build_pacman-apps.sh
../scripts/package_pacman-apps.sh
\end{lstlisting}
which is installed over the running system, on the PACMAN:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
root@Trenz:~# tar -xzf pacman-apps-v3_6_5-candidate.tar.gz -C /
root@Trenz:~# /etc/init.d/pacman_server restart
\end{lstlisting}

For rapid initial development, it can be useful to build the applications natively on the
development computer (in \path{petalinux/pkg/pacman-apps/files}, with the ZMQ development library
installed), which checks that they compile and runs the unit tests:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
make
./bin/pacman_units.elf
make clean
\end{lstlisting}
If you are certain that you know what you are doing, \texttt{make simulated-pacman} builds the
servers with a simulated PACMAN: DATA words sent to the command server return from the data server,
without hardware, for testing DAQ clients on the development computer.  Register access and the
board tools are not simulated.

\subsection{Bare-Metal Applications}

Bare-metal applications are built with \texttt{xsct} from \texttt{pacman-software/}, from the same
hardware export, and run over JTAG.  For example, the menu (on the common drivers,
Section~\ref{sec:drivers}):
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
xsct apps/menu/install.tcl
cd menu
xsct run.tcl
cd ..
\end{lstlisting}
The console is the UART of the JTAG/UART adapter, for example
\texttt{busybox microcom -s 115200 /dev/ttyUSB1}.  \texttt{apps/fwcheck} and \texttt{apps/hello}
build and run the same way.

\section{Quality Assurance and Quality Control}

The PACMAN card quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) regimen
relies on several complementary techniques:
\begin{itemize}

\item {\bf Loopback:} Loopback is generally the most effective method
  of feature verification.  The loopback mindset first drives the
  design toward adequate feedback and test input mechanisms, and then
  tests both the feature and its corresponding feedback or test input
  mechanism simultaneously.  Loopback paths in hardware test
  components and solder quality through the entire path.

\item {\bf Fault Localization:} Designing for fault localization
  forces a modular design that lends itself to effective QC.  For an
  important example, loopback can be performed in the hardware, or in
  firmware (just prior to leaving or entering the device), or in the
  CPU, so that the source of a failure can immediately be localized to
  hardware, programmable logic, or software.

\item {\bf Design Review:} The PACMAN design team includes a lead
  engineer, a consulting engineer, and a principal investigator.
  All three understand the board down to the last netlist.  The
  PACMAN design is also periodically subjected to independent review.

\item {\bf Prototype Systems:} The most effective form of quality
  control is operation in a working system, and the PACMAN card has
  been used continuously in prototype systems since the first version
  in 2020.

\end{itemize}

\subsection{Firmware Validation}

The PACMAN design rules insist that every custom RTL module in the design has a test bench
module which tests the core features of the module, at least to the level of the initial hardware
checkout.  The test benches use ASCII output.  Every module is simulated with GHDL before being used
in a Vivado project.  The test benches for the top-level units (e.g.\ RX, TX, ATC) are typically more
complicated, and test integration.  An example running the test benches for the tx unit:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
cd ghdl/tx
make
make run-testbenches
make run-unit-testbench
cd ../..
\end{lstlisting}
The module testbenches are quite comprehensible, especially while editing module code, but they
perform no automated checks.  We have complementary agent test benches, which are much more thorough
and automated.  The firmware requirements are presented in Appendix~\ref{sec:requirements}.  For each requirement,
an AI agent devises specific tests to validate the requirement in simulation and writes the test
bench.  The entire suite is run through a Makefile:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
cd ghdl/agents
make check
cd ../..
\end{lstlisting}
At present this runs about 15\,000 checks of 82 requirements (in about 25\,s), making this a strong complement
to more limited human testing.

Once the firmware is installed on a device, the 50 requirements marked sim+hw are also checked in the installed
firmware, with \texttt{pacman\_fwcheck} (J5 removed, tile power and every tile off):
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
root@Trenz:~#  /etc/init.d/pacman_server stop
root@Trenz:~#  pacman_fwcheck
root@Trenz:~#  /etc/init.d/pacman_server start
\end{lstlisting}

BREAKOUT TEST BOARD'', Fig.~\ref{fig:breakout}), that plugs into the PACMAN 400-pin connector and breaks out each
tile into a 2$\times$17 header.  Each breakout test board has its headers wired for either the loopback or the ATC
tests, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:test-cards}, and the two are swapped between runs.

The PACMAN-specific hardware is checked with \texttt{pacman\_hwcheck}, with the ASIC protection jumper (J5)
removed, no ASICs connected, and the PACMAN servers stopped.  The tests are chosen on the command line, and each
board gets its own run, with \texttt{-{}-tile\_mask} set to the tiles that carry it (bit $t$ for tile $t+1$;  the
default \hex{3ff} is all ten tiles).  With the loopback board installed (tile supplies into their loads, and the
UART loopback):
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
root@Trenz:~#  /etc/init.d/pacman_server stop
root@Trenz:~#  pacman_hwcheck --read_only --power --loopback --adc --i2c_stress
\end{lstlisting}
With the ATC board installed in its place (each tile's G and H looped to its PISO\_0 and PISO\_1, its CLK
to its analog monitor pair), and a 1\,kHz TTL signal on both LEMO inputs:
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
root@Trenz:~#  pacman_hwcheck --atc_outputs --ana_mon --lemo_inputs
\end{lstlisting}
Then the checks that need the operator:  the LEDs blink in turn, and the MUX places R28 across the SMA jacks
(J1001, J14) for a DMM reading (100\,$\Omega$, nominal).  The operator records what was seen:  the verdict covers
only what the program itself checks.
\begin{lstlisting}[frame=leftline]
root@Trenz:~#  pacman_hwcheck --blink --sma
root@Trenz:~#  /etc/init.d/pacman_server start
\end{lstlisting}
Each run ends with a \texttt{VERDICT} line (\texttt{PASS}, \texttt{FAIL}, or \texttt{INCOMPLETE} when something was
skipped), and \texttt{pacman\_hwcheck -{}-help} lists the tests and options.  With J5 installed, only
\texttt{-{}-read\_only} runs:  install J5 again once the checkout is done, before the card goes into a production
system or is connected to ASICs.
